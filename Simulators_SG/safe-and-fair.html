Safe and fair: control designs and biased data

Two small experiments with made-up numbers. First, a delivery robot meets problems (lost network, blinded camera): compare three designs for what it does next. Second, a hiring AI learns from old data: see how a skewed history turns into an unfair result.

What should the robot do when something breaks?

A delivery robot makes 1,000 trips. On some trips a problem appears (the network drops or mud blinds the camera). Choose what the robot does then.

Rules of this simple model: trips without a problem are on time. A guesses, so a share of the guesses (the number you chose) ends in an accident. B stops and waits about 10 minutes for the problem to go away, so the delivery is late but nobody is hurt. C stops and asks a human, who answers after the reply time, so the delivery is late by that time, and every problem uses one operator call.

What to notice
  • With the start settings (busy pavement, 10% of trips with a problem, 2 minutes), design A has 20 accidents in 1,000 trips; B and C have none but 100 late deliveries.
  • Design C is late by only 2 minutes instead of 10, but it needs 100 operator calls (about 3.3 hours of an operator's time). Nothing is free: safety costs time or people.
  • Change the street to a crowded school street: A gets worse (40 accidents), B and C do not change. The higher the stakes, the more a guess costs.
  • Slide the reply time to 10: C is no faster than B. A human only helps if the human answers in time.
  • The model is simple: a stopped robot could still be bumped, and a real operator can make mistakes. It shows the idea, not real numbers.

What does the hiring AI learn from old data?

A company trains an AI on the CVs of the people it hired in the past. Then 100 men and 100 women apply. Both groups have exactly the same skills (scores spread evenly from 0 to 100), so a fair AI should hire the same share of each.

Rules of this simple model: the AI hires anyone with a score of 70 or more. From the old data it learns a hidden bonus for men: bonus (points) = (share of men − 50) ÷ 2. The fairness test (red teaming) asks: is the women's hiring rate at least 80% of the men's rate?

What to notice
  • With fair data (50% men) there is no bonus and both groups are hired at 30%.
  • With the old data (90% men) the bonus is 20 points: 50% of the men but only 30% of the women are hired, although their skills are the same. Women ÷ men is 0.60, so the fairness test fails.
  • The AI is not "evil": it only copied the pattern of its data. Moving the slider back towards 50% (more diverse data) shrinks the gap.
  • The test passes up to 65% men in the data (women ÷ men is exactly 0.80). The exact limit is a rule of thumb, not a law of nature.
  • Real bias is harder to see than here: the AI may use clues like the name of a school or a hobby instead of a "male" column. This model shows the idea, not a real system.
